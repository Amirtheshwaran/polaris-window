Moon Missions for Classrooms

Nine missions of about 5 minutes each, for ages 10 and up. Every button opens the app at exactly the right place and date, using real NASA data. Read the question, explore, then reveal the answer.

Big idea: at the Moon's south pole the Sun never rises high. It slides around the horizon like a never-ending sunset. Mountains can block the Sun (no solar power) and the Earth (no radio contact). Astronauts need both.

1The crater that never sees the Sun

Open mission 1

This map colors the ground by how often it is sunlit in 2027. Find Shackleton crater, the big round crater next to the blue pin. What color is its floor? What does that mean?

Reveal answerThe floor is black: it got 0% sunlight all year. Some crater floors near the pole have stayed dark for billions of years. They are colder than about −170 °C, cold enough to trap frozen water. That ice is a big reason we are going back to the Moon.

2Chase the Sun

Open mission 2

Press ▶ at the bottom to make time run. Watch the shadows move and the gold Sun path in the panorama. What is the highest the Sun ever gets above the horizon here? Does it ever set?

Reveal answerOnly about 1.5 degrees, which is less than the width of your thumb held at arm's length! The Moon's axis is tilted only 1.5°, so at the pole the Sun just circles the horizon once a month. On this high ridge the Sun is visible most of the time, because the ground around it is lower.

3Phone home

Open mission 3

Look at the blue Earth path in the panorama for Malapert Massif. The Earth does not circle around like the Sun. What does it do instead? When it dips below the gray mountains, what happens to radio contact?

Reveal answerEarth bobs up and down in almost the same spot. The Moon always shows the same face to Earth, but it wobbles a little (called libration), so Earth rises and sinks over about a month. When Earth goes behind the terrain, astronauts lose direct radio contact. That's called a comms blackout.

4Be the mission planner

Open mission 4

Your mission lasts 14 days. Click Find best landing windows. Which site and date win? Now try the ☀️ Most power and 📡 Always talk to Earth buttons. Does the winner change? Why?

Reveal answerDifferent goals pick different places! A ridge near the pole wins for power because the Sun is almost always visible. Sites farther from the pole, like Mons Mouton, see Earth higher in the sky, so they win for communication. Real mission planners have to balance both, plus safe, flat ground.

5Find a safe parking spot

Open mission 5

This map shows only places that are flat enough to land (slope under 8°), colored by how often they have both sunlight and Earth in view. Click on the brightest spot you can find to drop your own landing site. How does your site compare with Connecting Ridge?

Reveal answerThe brightest places are narrow ridges and crater rims. Good landing spots are rare and small, and that's why choosing a site is so hard. Use ⚖️ Compare to put your site next to the official ones.

6Check our work like a NASA scientist

Open mission 6

Scientists always check their results. Look at the two maps: one made by NASA, one made by Polaris Window. Do they match? Then go to 🧍 Stand at site and click ✅ verify in JPL Horizons to compare our Sun height with NASA's own calculator.

Reveal answerThey match very closely (correlation 0.99 for both sunlight and Earth visibility, where 1.0 would be perfect), and the Sun height matches JPL Horizons to about a thousandth of a degree. Checking against independent data is how scientists build trust in their results.

7Moon detective: what happened to Athena?

Open mission 7

In March 2025 a real robot lander called Athena (part of NASA's CLPS program) landed near Mons Mouton. Look at the panorama and the numbers on the day it landed. Was there enough sunlight? Could it see Earth? Then read the "True story" box. Why did the mission end after only about a day?

Reveal answerThe place and the date were great: sunshine and Earth in view the whole time. But Athena came down inside a small crater, too small to show on these 80-meter maps, and tipped onto its side, so its solar panels could not charge. Engineers need both: the right place and time (like this tool shows) and very detailed maps of rocks and craters.

8Keep your lander alive!

Open mission 8

Scroll down to 🔋 Will your lander survive? Your lander lands on Malapert Massif on 10 April 2027 with a 40 kWh battery, and the battery runs out! Try three fixes: (1) make the battery bigger, (2) make the solar panel bigger, (3) change the landing date with the timeline at the bottom. What is the smallest battery that survives 14 days? Which fix is easiest?

Reveal answerThe lander dies because it lands just before a long lunar night. Solar panels can't help in the dark, so you either need a much bigger battery (enough to power the lander for days) or a better landing date that starts with sunshine. Picking the right date is often the cheapest fix, and that's exactly why planners use tools like this one.

9Read the Moon's weather forecast

Open mission 9

Scroll down to the 📆 12-month landing forecast for Malapert Massif. Every square is one day. Green means sunshine and Earth in view all day. Dark red means night. Which months have the longest runs of green? Which months would be the worst time to land? Click a green day and check the panorama.

Reveal answerThe dark red nights bunch up in the middle of the year (around April to July in 2027), while other months have long green runs. That's the Moon's "seasons" at the pole: the Sun's path tilts a little over the year, so sometimes hills block it for days. A good mission plan lands at the start of a long green run.
